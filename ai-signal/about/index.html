A note from the desk

Interesting is not the same as true.

AI Signal is a publication within Dugi Research Lab. Its purpose is to make technical change understandable without flattening every announcement into a breakthrough—or dismissing every ambitious idea as hype.

Launch edition: demonstration content. The initial stories are fictional editorial exercises created with AI assistance. They do not report current events, quote real people, or assign ratings to real companies. Map directions are examples.

Three distinct kinds of statement

  1. Verified facts: statements tied to identifiable sources, with verification dates. A source's claim is not automatically independent proof.
  2. Interpretation: technical and business implications, with the reasoning and assumptions visible.
  3. Outlook: conditional possibilities and what would need to happen for them to become plausible. Never presented as an observed outcome.

Signal strength is editorial judgment.

These labels describe our assessment of significance. They are not a measurement, a confidence percentage, or investment advice.

  • Noise: insufficient evidence or little actionable change in the story as presented. Not a claim that an idea or researcher has no value.
  • Interesting: a development or question worth investigating; practical significance remains unclear.
  • Important: a material change for an identifiable set of users, if supported by the evidence.
  • Structural Shift: a potential change in the constraints or incentives shaping a wider system. A strong label that requires a clear argument.

The Signal Map is directional.

Every arrow names a dimension, supplies a rationale, and links to relevant coverage where available. Rising governance requirements and falling inference costs refer to different things. They cannot be combined into a single score. The launch map is entirely illustrative.

Before a real story is published

  1. Collect primary sources and preserve their dates and provenance.
  2. Separate the reported event from claims made by the source.
  3. Verify the fact statements and document what remains uncertain.
  4. Write analysis and outlook in their own fields.
  5. Record human editorial approval, then build and review the resulting page.

The builder requires sources, linked fact records, verification dates, and recorded approval before a story can be marked as published reporting. A machine can validate those fields; it cannot establish that a human actually did the review.

Corrections and ownership

Dugi Selmanaj maintains the publication. Future factual corrections should identify what changed and why in the article, update its last-updated date, and retain the repository history. To flag an issue, use the publication repository.

A static publication, for now.

Stories are stored as structured JSON and transformed into standalone HTML. Reading does not require JavaScript; search and filters use a small local script. No news collector, model pipeline, subscriber database, analytics, or scheduled publication is running.

This site has no application tracking or form submission. GitHub Pages may keep ordinary hosting request logs. Read the content JSON · Inspect the source.
